The Matrix

Which Stack layer carries each Stance.

The Stances describe the contract a human thinks they have with an AI system: “I am asking an Oracle”, “I am handing work to an Executor”. The Stack describes what the human must actually supply for any contract to hold: instruction, information, purpose, doubt, consistency, measurement, and a place for the work to run.

Each stance leans on one layer more than the others. That layer is where the stance’s characteristic failure lives, and therefore where the guardrail belongs. Knowing which stance you are in tells you which layer to check first. That is mode fluency in one move.

§3 how_to_use

How to use it

  1. Before you start: name the stance out loud. Then look up its layer and do the one thing in the guardrail column.
  2. When it goes wrong: do not rewrite the prompt first. Find the stance you were in, go to its layer, and re-enter there.
  3. For a team: the stances your team uses most tell you which layers your shared setup must make strong. A team that delegates (Executor) needs harness and judgment work; a team that researches (Librarian, Interpreter) needs evaluation.

§4 axis 03

Autonomy: how much of the work do I watch?

As autonomy increases, spec rigor must increase proportionally. In Assistive mode, your brain is the spec. In Autonomous mode, the written spec is the only control mechanism between intent and execution.

Modes, from The Complete Guide to Specifying Work for AI (revised with feedback from Cory Gwin)
ModeHow it worksHuman understandingRisk of incorrect outputSpec rigor required
AssistiveYou type, AI suggestsVery HighVery LowLow
DirectedYou describe, AI types, you reviewHighLow–MediumMedium
AutonomousYou assign, AI executes, you review the resultMediumMedium–HighHigh
Autonomous + SpecAutonomous, with a written specMediumMedium (reduced)High (by definition)
ContinuousAI runs as a background serviceLowHighVery High

Adding a spec is the only intervention in the table that improves a risk rating. The smallest spec is the four-line Intent Doc: TASK (what specifically was asked for), SCOPE (who or what is affected, and excluded), BOUNDARIES (what the AI will not do), IF UNCERTAIN (ask, flag, or stop).

As autonomy rises, the stances shift toward the Executor and the load shifts toward Harness, Judgment and Evaluation. This is why the AI @ Workplace curriculum spends 101–301 on chatbots and 401 onward on agents: the layer that carries the load moves as the human steps back. Productive people also switch modes on a single task; staying in one mode regardless of the task is its own anti-pattern (mode rigidity).

§5 axis 04

Ambition: how big is the ask?

adopts Felix Rieseberg's four types of ambition, from “How to be more ambitious: A tutorial for humans with models” (13 October 2026). The descriptions below are his.

01 ambition

Speed

The same work in less time, the bread and butter for AI's economic value. Well established and requires little creativity in how you approach AI.

02 ambition

Altitude

Going up in abstraction and considering the outcome rather than the individual task.

03 ambition

Depth

Considering a task the way a field-leading expert would. Rather than operating at the task's layer, we go one level deeper and touch the underlying data, tools, and problems.

04 ambition

Range

Drawing on multiple fields rather than just the one the task might need at the surface, using knowledge, tools, and techniques you normally wouldn't.

§6 lineage

Lineage

The stances grew out of an earlier set of archetypes of LLM-human interaction, now published in full as The Fourteen Stances. Related framings classify by autonomy or social role rather than by the human's expectation: Anthropic's AI Fluency (4Ds: Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence), Mollick's centaurs and cyborgs, Microsoft's “agent boss”, and the CHI 2026 “human-LLM archetypes” paper for decision support (arXiv 2602.11924). The Stack's intent engineering layer is credited to Nate B. Jones.
