## 1. Calculators in math education

**Timeline**

- **1970s debates.** No primary 1970s source retrieved. The 1975 CBMS/NACOME *Overview and Analysis of School Mathematics K-12* appears in NCTM reference lists [7]; its calculator position is **UNVERIFIED**.
- **1980, NCTM *An Agenda for Action*** [1]. Eight recommendations for the 1980s. The first three:
  1. "problem solving should be the focus of school mathematics";
  2. "basic skills ... should be defined to encompass more than computational facility";
  3. "mathematics programs should take full advantage of the power of calculators and computers at all grade levels" (ERIC abstract of the document).

  The machine takes the arithmetic; the human curriculum moves up to problem solving.
- **1989, NCTM *Curriculum and Evaluation Standards for School Mathematics*** [2]. 54 standards across K-4, 5-8, 9-12 and evaluation. Its 1991 Addenda series describes the themes as "problem solving, reasoning, communication, and connections" (ERIC ED339617, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED339617).
- **1990s, "math wars."** Schoenfeld [6] traces these controversies directly to the 1989 Standards:
  - traditionalists feared "standards-based" curricula were "superficial";
  - reformers claimed "a deeper, richer view of mathematics."

  The underlying questions (elite vs. mass mathematics, excellence vs. equity) are "more than a century old"; calculators were a flashpoint, not the root.
- **Graphing calculators.** Ellington 2006 [5]: 42 studies, middle school to first-semester calculus.
- **Current state.** On the digital SAT, "a built-in Desmos calculator is available for the entire Math section", with scientific and graphing modes [10]. The tool is now assumed in high-stakes testing, not merely permitted.

**What the research found**

- **Hembree & Dessart 1986** [3]: meta-analysis of 79 research reports, precollege (K-12).
  - At all grades except Grade 4, calculator use "in concert with traditional mathematics instruction apparently improves the average student's basic skills with paper and pencil".
  - "Sustained calculator use in Grade 4 appears to hinder the development of basic skills in average students."
  - Attitude and self-concept were better across grades.
  - Population/method: synthesis of mostly 1970s-80s school studies (Glass effect sizes). It measures school achievement, not adult work.
- **Ellington 2003** [4]: meta-analysis of 54 precollege studies.
  - Operational and problem-solving skills "improved when calculators were an integral part of testing and instruction."
  - Results were "mixed when calculators were not part of assessment, but in all cases, calculator use did not hinder the development of mathematical skills."
  - Retention and transfer flagged as unstudied: the long-run question is open.
- **Ellington 2006** [5]: 42 graphing-calculator studies.
  - Students benefited when calculators were used in instruction but not testing.
  - When calculators were used in both testing and instruction, procedural, conceptual and overall achievement improved.
- **NAEP 2000 (correlational)**, as summarised by NCTM president Johnny Lott in November 2002 [7]:
  - frequent calculator use by **fourth graders** was "associated with lower average mathematics scores";
  - for eighth and twelfth graders, "more frequent calculator usage was associated with higher scores."

  Association, not causation; the pro-calculator body itself conceded the Grade-4 signal.

**How curricula shifted toward quantitative literacy**

- Lynn Arthur Steen (ed.), *Mathematics and Democracy: The Case for Quantitative Literacy* (NCED/MAA, 2001) [8]. Its central claim, as quoted by Grawe (2012) [9]: "The world of the twenty-first century is a world awash in numbers".
- Grawe summarises the book's distinction: quantitative literacy (QL) is "built on a foundation of mathematical skill" but is "distinct from traditional mathematics". "Where mathematics is intentionally abstract, QL drives toward the specific context of problems" [9].
- The MAA PDF returned 403; the quotation is via Grawe (**primary not re-read**).

**What was lost (evidence)**

- The only consistent harm signal is at **early grades**: Hembree's Grade 4 finding [3] and the NAEP 2000 Grade 4 correlation [7].
- No high-quality evidence found of broad adult arithmetic loss; retention studies are missing [4].
- **Defensible lesson:** the tool was net-positive when (a) foundational skill came first and (b) assessment was redesigned around the tool. Unconsidered early substitution was the risk.

## 2. Aviation automation

**Automation complacency and automation bias.** Parasuraman & Manzey 2010 [11] is a review of empirical studies in *Human Factors*:

- Complacency "occurs under conditions of multiple-task load, when manual tasks compete with the automated task for the operator's attention".
- It "is found in both naive and expert participants and cannot be overcome with simple practice".
- Automation bias produces "omission and commission errors when decision aids are imperfect". It "cannot be prevented by training or instructions" and "can affect decision making in individuals as well as in teams".

**This is decision-relevant: the literature says instruction alone does not cure automation bias. Design and procedure must carry part of the load.**

**"Children of the magenta line."** This is a 1997 American Airlines training talk, attributed in secondary sources to Capt. Warren VanderBurgh [12].

- OPSGROUP (Mark Zee, 2021) calls it "perhaps the first aviation meme". It summarises the premise as pilots relying "on automation so much that they can't fly the airplane anymore", with the line "You can't call yourself a pilot unless you can turn it all off and fly it safely" [12].
- Original video not reached; speaker and date come from journalism/blog comments (**UNVERIFIED**; do not quote the talk verbatim).

**Air France 447 (1 June 2009; BEA final report, July 2012)** [13]

- Sequence: Pitot icing caused the autopilot to disconnect and reconfigure to alternate law, followed by "inappropriate control inputs that destabilized the flight path". The crew "never understood that they were stalling".
- Contributing factors include:
  - "the absence of any training, at high altitude, in manual aeroplane handling";
  - task-sharing weakened by "incomprehension of the situation" and the "startle effect";
  - Flight Director cues "that may led the crew to believe that their actions were appropriate".
- On training, the BEA says: "Current training practices ... limit the pilots' abilities to acquire or maintain basic airmanship skills."
- On the safety model: if the crew's "capacity for initial mastery and then diagnosis is lost, the safety model is then in 'common failure mode'".

**Asiana 214 (6 July 2013; NTSB AAR-14/01, adopted 24 June 2014)** [14]

- Probable cause: mismanagement of the descent, "the pilot flying's unintended deactivation of automatic airspeed control", inadequate airspeed monitoring and a delayed go-around.
- Contributing: autothrottle and autopilot flight director (AFDS) complexities, "inadequately described in Boeing's documentation and Asiana's pilot training, which increased the likelihood of mode error".
- Findings:
  - mode-awareness degradation from "role confusion";
  - poor monitoring from "expectancy, increased workload, fatigue, and automation reliance".
- On skill atrophy, the board notes the pilot flying had 9,684 hours, yet "lacked critical manual flying skills. This demonstrates that without greater opportunity for pilots to manually fly the airplane, their airplane handling skills degrade."

**FAA PARC/CAST Flight Deck Automation Working Group (letter dated 8 Sept 2013)** [15]. 29 findings and 18 recommendations, based on worldwide accident, incident and normal-operations data plus interviews.

- **Finding 1:** "Pilots mitigate safety and operational risks on a frequent basis, and the aviation system is designed to rely on that mitigation."
- **Finding 2:** vulnerabilities in manual flight skills, including "retention of such skills".
- **Finding 4:** pilots "sometimes rely too much on automated systems and may be reluctant to intervene"; "Autoflight mode confusion errors continue to occur."
- Trainers expressed "uncertainty as to how these skills may decay"; "a lack of consensus over what skills underpin manual handling." Recommendations 1-2: manual flight operations, mode awareness.

**Crew Resource Management (CRM) origins**

- NASA/Industry Workshop "Resource Management on the Flight Deck", San Francisco, 26-28 June 1979. Proceedings: NASA CP-2120 (Cooper, White & Lauber, March 1980) [16].
- A NASA retrospective (Kanki & Rosekind 1996) says that in the 1970s safety professionals "became profoundly concerned about the prevalence of crew-caused accidents". They answered with crew concepts ("flightdeck management, crew coordination, and cockpit resource management") taught as a "grassroots" culture change [17].
- The claim that the workshop *coined* "CRM" is widely repeated but was not confirmed in the NTRS record (**UNVERIFIED**).

**Pilot certificate ladder (US, 14 CFR Part 61; eCFR current text)** [18]

| Step | Requirement | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| Student pilot | Training status | May not act as pilot in command (PIC) carrying passengers, for compensation or hire, or in furtherance of a business (§61.89) |
| Private | Minimum 40 hours of flight time, including 20 dual and 10 solo, for airplane single-engine (§61.109) | Passengers allowed, but no PIC "for compensation or hire" (§61.113) |
| Instrument rating | A *rating* added to a certificate. Requires a private certificate, 50 hours of cross-country PIC time and 40 hours of actual or simulated instrument time (§61.65) | Flight by reference to instruments: operating when you cannot see outside |
| Commercial | Age 18 (§61.123); 250 hours (§61.129) | May act as PIC "carrying persons or property for compensation or hire" (§61.133) |
| Airline transport pilot (ATP) | Age 23, or 21 under §61.160 (§61.153); 1,500 hours total time (§61.159) | Top certificate |

Graded privileges tied to demonstrated experience; paid work gated behind commercial.

**Pilot-in-command rule, 14 CFR 91.3** [19]

- (a) "The pilot in command of an aircraft is directly responsible for, and is the final authority as to, the operation of that aircraft."
- (b) In an emergency the PIC "may deviate from any rule of this part to the extent required".
- (c) Deviations are reported on request. Responsibility is non-delegable, whatever is engaged.

## 3. From "computer literacy" to "computational thinking"

**Papert, *Mindstorms* (1980)** [20]. Papert rejects the shallow reading of "computer literacy":

> "This phrase is often taken as meaning knowing how to program, or knowing about the varied uses made of computers. But true computer literacy is not just knowing how to make use of computers and computational ideas. It is knowing when it is appropriate to do so." (c. p. 155 of the 1st-ed. scan)

He also uses "computational thinking" ("how to integrate computational thinking into everyday life"; c. pp. 182-183), and frames "think like a computer" as "a powerful addition to a person's stock of mental tools", rejecting that "one must give up an old method in order to adopt a new one". Page numbers are from scan footers; confirm against print.

**Wing (2006), CACM 49(3):33-35** [21]

- "Computational thinking is a fundamental skill for everyone, not just for computer scientists. To reading, writing, and arithmetic, we should add computational thinking to every child's analytical ability."
- Defining contrasts: "Conceptualizing, not programming"; "Fundamental, not rote skill"; "A way that humans, not computers, think"; "Ideas, not artifacts".
- The end state: CT "will be a reality when it is so integral to human endeavors it disappears as an explicit philosophy."

**Entry into national curricula (England)**

- The Royal Society's January 2012 report *Shut down or restart?* (summary PDF read directly) [22] found:
  - computing education in many UK schools was "highly unsatisfactory";
  - many pupils "gain nothing beyond basic digital literacy skills such as how to use a word-processor or a database";
  - teaching was "biased towards 'how to use office software'".
- Its Recommendation 1 is a naming decision: "The term ICT as a brand should be reviewed", with disaggregation into "digital literacy, Information Technology and Computer Science" (by analogy with basic literacy / English Language / English Literature). It adds: "The term 'ICT' should no longer be used as it has attracted too many negative connotations." 
- The Department for Education's computing programmes of study were published 11 September 2013. Their purpose statement opens: "A high-quality computing education equips pupils to use computational thinking and creativity to understand and change the world"; it names computer science as "the core" and digital literacy as one outcome [23].
- Teaching from September 2014 per secondary copies (confirm before print). Other countries not researched.

**Caution: CT's vagueness critique.** Denning (2017), "Remaining trouble spots with computational thinking" (CACM) [24], addresses "unresolved questions concerning computational thinking". A successful name can still be criticised as under-defined. Abstract only; specific critiques **UNVERIFIED**.

**Lesson for naming a discipline (evidence-based, not a brand proposal)**

- The successful rename moved the name from the **tool** ("computer", "ICT") to the **thinking** ("computational thinking").
- The old name was explicitly retired because it had come to mean "how to use office software" [22]. A tool-operation label can become a stigma once the tool is commonplace.
- It explicitly subordinated the operational layer: "digitally literate" became one outcome inside a larger discipline [23].
- Papert's version already bundled *how* with *when*: "knowing when it is appropriate".

## 4. Direct AI-era evidence that the human is now the bottleneck

1. **METR, July 2025 RCT** [25].
   - *Shows:* AI increased completion time 19% (CI +2% to +39%); developers forecast −24% and afterwards believed −20%.
   - *Population/method:* 16 experienced OSS developers, 246 randomised tasks in mature repos they knew (avg 5 years); Cursor Pro, Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet; Feb-June 2025.
   - *Does not show:* effects for novices, greenfield work, or current tools.
2. **METR, February 2026 design update** [26].
   - *Shows:* the 19% slowdown should not be presented as the current state.
   - *Population/method:* follow-up with 57 developers, 143 repos, 800+ tasks. Point estimates were −18% task time for returning developers (CI −38% to +9%) and −4% for new recruits (CI −15% to +9%).
   - *Caveat:* METR calls the data "an unreliable signal": developers increasingly refused to work without AI (selection bias). Both intervals include zero.
3. **Budzyń et al., Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol, Oct 2025** [27].
   - *Shows:* non-AI adenoma detection rate fell 28.4% (226/795) to 22.4% (145/648) after AI introduction (−6.0 points, p=0.0089; OR 0.69).
   - *Population/method:* retrospective observational study at four Polish centres within the ACCEPT trial; 1,443 non-AI colonoscopies, 3 months before vs. 3 months after AI rollout.
   - *Does not show:* causation (observational), or the long-run trajectory.
   - **Anomaly:** a 2026 scoping review in *ESMO Real World Data & Digital Oncology* calls this "a multicenter randomized trial" [28]. That is a misdescription; cite the original.
4. **Kosmyna et al. (MIT Media Lab), "Your Brain on ChatGPT", arXiv June 2025** [29].
   - *Shows:* the LLM group showed the weakest EEG connectivity and the lowest essay ownership, and struggled to quote their own essays.
   - *Population/method:* 54 participants writing essays in LLM / search / brain-only conditions over three sessions; 18 completed a fourth, crossover session.
   - *Does not show:* workplace effects, or durable harm. It is a small, lab-based preprint; peer-review status **UNVERIFIED**.
5. **Lee et al. (Microsoft Research and CMU), CHI 2025** [30].
   - *Shows:* "higher confidence in GenAI is associated with less critical thinking", while higher self-confidence is associated with more. Critical thinking shifts toward "information verification, response integration, and task stewardship".
   - *Population/method:* survey of 319 knowledge workers, 936 self-reported examples.
   - *Does not show:* measured (rather than self-reported) critical-thinking change.
6. **Shen & Tamkin (Anthropic), 29 Jan 2026 RCT** [31].
   - *Shows:* AI group scored 50% vs. 67% on the follow-up quiz (d=0.738, p=0.01), largest gap on debugging; speed gain not significant; those who asked AI for explanations retained more.
   - *Population/method:* 52 mostly junior engineers learning an unfamiliar Python library (Trio), then quizzed.
   - *Does not show:* effects for agentic tools (the authors expect larger effects there), or long-term outcomes.
7. **Anthropic Economic Index, Feb 2025** [32].
   - *Shows:* AI use leaned toward augmentation (57%) over automation (43%).
   - *Population/method:* privacy-preserving analysis of Claude.ai conversations mapped to O*NET tasks.
   - *Does not show:* outcomes or quality; it covers one vendor's users only.
8. **Anthropic Economic Index, 15 Sept 2025** [33].
   - *Shows:* "Directive" conversations (full delegation) rose from 27% to 39%: "the first report where automation usage exceeds augmentation usage". First-party API (business) use was 77% automation.
   - *Population/method:* same method, eight months later.
   - *Does not show:* whether that delegation was competent. Its trend direction is exactly the Executor archetype's risk.
9. **Dell'Acqua et al. (HBS/BCG), "Jagged Technological Frontier", 2023** [34].
   - *Shows:* on 18 inside-frontier tasks, users completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, at higher quality. On one task chosen to be outside the frontier, users were 19 percentage points less likely to be correct.
   - *Population/method:* preregistered experiment with 758 BCG consultants using GPT-4.
   - *Does not show:* current-model frontiers, or non-consulting work.
10. **Microsoft Work Trend Index, 23 Apr 2025** [35].
    - *Shows:* "every employee becomes an agent boss"; 82% of leaders "confident that they'll use digital labor to expand workforce capacity in the next 12-18 months"; 67% of leaders vs. 40% of employees familiar with agents; 36% expect teams "managing" agents within five years; proposes a "human-agent ratio".
    - *Population/method:* survey of 31,000 workers in 31 countries, plus LinkedIn and Microsoft 365 telemetry.
    - *Does not show:* measured productivity; vendor survey, cite as sentiment.

## 5. Synthesis: what transfers and what does not (≤400 words)

**Transfers**

- **Calculators:** the field moved the human curriculum *up* to problem solving and modeling once the machine took computation [1][2]. Benefits were strongest when the tool was built into assessment, not bolted on [4][5]. Harm appeared where tools displaced foundations early [3][7]. Hence: fundamentals before delegation; redesign evaluation around the tool.
- **Aviation:**
  - complacency is attentional, affects experts, and instruction does not cure it [11], so structural checkpoints are required;
  - skills degrade without deliberate manual practice [14][15];
  - mode confusion is the closest cockpit analog of "which layer failed" [14][15];
  - the system is *designed to rely on* human mitigation [15];
  - responsibility is legally non-delegable [19];
  - CRM made communication a team discipline [16][17];
  - competence is graded into privileges [18].
- **CT:** the successful name described thinking rather than the tool, and placed tool literacy inside it [21][23]. Papert's definition already joined *how* with *when* [20].

**Negative transfer (disanalogies)**

1. **Determinism.** Calculators return the same correct answer every time; LLMs are stochastic and fluently wrong, so one-time trust calibration fails.
2. **Failure signalling.** AF447's autopilot disconnected and warnings sounded [13]. LLM errors usually come with no alarm and read as confident prose. Autopilots do not hallucinate; their closest failure (silent mode change, Asiana [14]) is narrow and documented.
3. **Scope of delegation.** Calculators automate a well-defined sub-skill *below* the problem. LLMs reach into framing, reasoning and writing, the "math by hand" layer itself, so there is no clean line beneath which delegation is safe.
4. **Moving frontier.** Calculators were stable for decades; AI's frontier is jagged [34] and shifts within months [25][26], so teach diagnosis, not a fixed list of safe uses.
5. **Institutional scaffolding.** Aviation has licences, recurrent checks, simulators, a legal PIC rule and public accident reports [13]-[19]. Knowledge work has none of these, so accountability and the "simulator" must be self-imposed.
6. **Operator as designer.** Pilots do not program autopilot logic; AI users write their own prompts, skills and agents, so they are designer and operator at once.
7. **Thinking vs. tool.** CT is tool-independent [21]. "AI operation" is partly tool-specific and decays as tools change; only the stance and judgment layer is durable.
8. **Evidence base.** Calculator evidence is K-12 achievement [3][4]. AI deskilling evidence is adult, early and small-sample [27][29][31]. Claims should be sized accordingly.
