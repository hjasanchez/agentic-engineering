**Use: students and teachers both use AI; guidance and instruction lag.**
- US teens (Pew, fieldwork 25 Sept–9 Oct 2025, published 24 Feb 2026, n=1,458): 54% use chatbots for schoolwork; about 1 in 10
  do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbot help; 76% of teen users say students at their school use chatbots to cheat at least
  sometimes [16]†.
- US students and teachers (RAND, Sept 2025): 54% of students and 53% of teachers used AI for school by winter 2025; 45% of principals
  reported an AI policy; 19% of students reported any teacher-led instruction on AI; half worried about being falsely accused of cheating.
  RAND, March 2026: 62% of students used AI for homework in Dec 2025, and 67% said more AI use harms critical thinking [17]†.
- US teachers (Gallup–Walton, published June 2025, n=2,232): 60% used AI in 2024–25; weekly users report 5.9 h/week saved; 19% work in a
  school with an AI policy [18]†. Gallup 2026 (fieldwork 9 Feb–2 Mar 2026, n=2,069): **58% of teachers have no guidance on using AI for
  grading and student feedback**; 69% none on tutoring [19]†.
- UK undergraduates (HEPI): 92% used AI and 88% used it for assessments (Feb 2025, n=1,041); 95% used AI, 94% for assessed work, 12% put
  AI-generated text directly into assessed work, 15% use AI for companionship (Mar 2026, n=1,054). Only 36% had institutional support to
  build AI skills (2025) [20][21]†.
- University students on Claude (Anthropic, 8 Apr 2025, 574,740 conversations): ~47% "Direct" (answer-seeking) conversations; Claude's
  responses classed mostly as Creating (39.8%) and Analyzing (30.2%), i.e. higher-order work being handed over [22].

**Evidence: the design of the help decides the outcome, not access.**
- **Bastani et al., PNAS, July 2025** (field experiment, ~1,000 high-school maths students, Turkey, 2023–24): practice scores rose 48%
  (GPT Base, ChatGPT-like) and 127% (GPT Tutor, prompted not to give answers); on the later exam without AI, GPT Base students scored 17%
  *worse* than controls and GPT Tutor students the same as controls [23][24].
- **Kestin et al., Scientific Reports, June 2025** (Harvard intro physics, RCT crossover, n=194): a carefully scaffolded AI tutor with
  pre-written solutions produced median learning gains more than double those of in-class active learning, in a median 49 minutes; two
  lessons, short-term post-test, ceiling effects [25].
- **World Bank, Edo State, Nigeria** (six weeks after school, June–July 2024, with teacher support): about 0.3 SD, "roughly two years of
  typical learning"; working paper May 2025 [26].
- **Tutor CoPilot** (Wang et al., 2024–25, 900 tutors, 1,800 students): AI guidance to *human* tutors raised mastery 4 points, 9 points for
  students of lower-rated tutors [27]†.
- **Offloading signals:** Fan et al. (BJET 2025, n=117): ChatGPT improved essay scores but not knowledge gain or transfer ("metacognitive
  laziness") [28]†. Gerlich (Societies, Jan 2025, n=666, correlational): heavier AI use correlates with lower critical-thinking scores,
  mediated by offloading [29]†. Kosmyna et al. ("Your Brain on ChatGPT", MIT, arXiv June 2025, 54 participants, 18 in session 4): lowest
  EEG connectivity and essay ownership in the LLM group; still a preprint; a January 2026 comment (arXiv 2601.00856) argues for more
  conservative readings and flags reporting inconsistencies; the lead author says it does not show "brain rot" [30][31]†. Shen & Tamkin
  (Anthropic, Jan 2026, 52 junior engineers): AI group 50% vs 67% on the follow-up quiz; those who asked for explanations retained more (R4).
- **Detection is weak:** GPT detectors flagged TOEFL essays by non-native writers as AI-written at high rates (Liang et al., Patterns,
  July 2023; exact average rate varies by summary) [32]†.

**What teachers are offered (mostly free, mostly funded by AI labs or governments).**
- National Academy for AI Instruction (AFT + UFT, Microsoft $12.5m, OpenAI $10m, Anthropic $0.5m; $23m; July 2025): free to 1.8m AFT
  members, target 400,000 educators in five years [33]†.
- DfE modules (free, England) [7]†; Common Sense Education AI literacy lessons, grades 6–12, ≤20 minutes each, free [34]†; Experience AI
  (Raspberry Pi Foundation + Google DeepMind), free INSET days for secondary schools [35]†.
- ISTE+ASCD "AI Deep Dive for Educators", 15 h self-paced: $186 member, $249 non-member (Feb 2025 flyer), scholarships common [36]†.
- Khanmigo for Teachers free in the US (Microsoft-funded, 2024); districts pay per student ($15–35 quoted) [37]†. MagicSchool reports
  ~3m active educators [38]†; Brisk ($99.99/yr individual tier) sells AI essay feedback in Google Docs [38]†.
- UK INSET benchmarks: council traded rate £908/day (Lancashire 2024–25), commercial AI trainers £1,049–1,795/day; online AI session
  £75 per head (Learn Sheffield 2025) [39]†.

**What students are offered.** Built-in learning modes: Claude learning mode (Apr 2025 for education, all users Aug 2025), ChatGPT study
mode (29 July 2025), Gemini Guided Learning (6 Aug 2025) [40]†. Khanmigo for students via districts. Common Sense lessons via teachers.
Almost nothing addressed to the student as a user who chooses *what kind of help* to ask for.

**Who pays.** Districts and schools (procurement, data agreements), ministries and unions (free national programmes), universities (centres
for teaching and learning, faculty development), parents (tutoring), AI labs (as funders, which crowds out paid PD).

## 2. The gap, stated precisely

Henrique's observation has three parts. The evidence treats them differently.

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Schools either ban AI or let students use it indiscriminately | **Supported**, with a 2026 update: the two largest US districts restricted student AI in Sept 2026; most schools still have no policy. | [11][12]; 19% of teachers in a school with a policy (2025) [18]†; 45% of principals report one [17]† |
| Teachers use AI to review the AI work | **Plausible, not measured.** Teachers use AI for feedback and grading with no guidance (58%); students use AI for most assessed work (UK 94%). No survey measures the loop itself. | [19]†, [21]†, [38]† |
| Nobody is thinking deeply about it or managing the whole at a higher level | **Not supported as stated.** UNESCO, EU+OECD, DfE, JCQ, Sydney, the AIAS, Mollick's "Assigning AI: seven approaches" (tutor, coach, mentor, teammate, tool, simulator, student; 2023) [41]†, and the Bastani/Kestin designs are serious thinking. | §1 |

**The gap that survives, stated precisely:** the thinking exists at two levels that do not meet.
1. *Policy and assessment permission* (UNESCO, AIAS, two-lane, JCQ) say **how much** AI an assignment allows.
2. *Tool design* (study modes, GPT Tutor, Khanmigo) builds the Tutor stance **into the product** for the student.

What nobody hands the student and the teacher is the layer between: **a shared, subject-neutral way to name what kind of help you are
asking for at the moment of use, and who checks the result.** The student has no habit of choosing Tutor over Oracle when the tool
allows both (ChatGPT's study mode can be switched off in one click [40]†); the teacher has no protocol for checking AI-assisted work, or
their own AI-assisted grading, beyond "detect it", which is unreliable [32]†. Practice data points the same way: 19% of US students got
teacher-led AI instruction (2025) [17]†, 36% of UK undergraduates got institutional AI-skill support (2025) [20]†, 58% of US teachers have
no guidance on AI for grading (2026) [19]†.

**Why this is Mode Fluency's best external evidence in any domain.** Bastani is the cleanest field test of the stance claim anywhere: the
same model, two stances (Genie/Oracle vs Tutor): unrestricted answers hurt the later exam without AI (about 17% worse than no AI), the tutor with guardrails showed no significant difference; both raised practice scores (about +48% and +127%) [23]. The calculator lesson in the essay ("foundations first, then
the tool; redesign the checking around the tool") is native to teachers.

**What the evidence does not show (the honest limit).** In Bastani and Kestin the guardrails were **designed into the tool**. Nobody has
shown that students who *choose* the Tutor stance themselves, in an unrestricted tool, get the same protection. Shen & Tamkin (adults)
is the nearest hint: those who asked for explanations retained more. That is the untested claim a pilot could probe (§7).

## 3. Where Mode Fluency fits and where it does not

| Dimension | Fits | Does not fit |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Teachers (adults; best fit). University students. Upper secondary 16–18 with the teacher as the user of the material. | Under 13 (UNESCO floor; NYC bans K-8). Direct-to-minor products under Henrique's name. |
| Who | **Teachers as AI users** (planning, feedback, grading: the Interpreter and Executor stances, Evaluation and Judgment layers). Teachers as assignment designers. University teaching centres. | District-wide procurement. Building tutoring software (Khanmigo, MagicSchool, the labs own this). AI detection. |
| Subjects | Writing-heavy and project work: essays, coursework, lab reports, extended projects (EPQ, IB Extended Essay), research methods. Any subject where the process can be logged. | Drill-and-practice maths and early literacy: the calculator rule (foundations first) already answers it, and the tool-level guardrail is the right fix. |
| Stances | Tutor (adopt), Mirror (adopt, with the "structure, not agreement" rule), Adversary (adopt for drafts), Oracle and Librarian (with the uncertainty and citation checks), Interpreter (evidence trail). | Therapist/Confessor and companion use with minors: a safeguarding matter (15% of UK students use AI for companionship [21]†; NYC bans companion bots [11]). Executor for students under 16. |
| Institutions | EU schools as **deployers** under AI Act Art. 4 (literacy measures) — my reading, not legal advice: the Article 4 kit idea in R11 transfers. Independent and international schools (faster buyers). | Anything that evaluates learning outcomes (Annex III high-risk) or reads emotion (banned). |

**Tool access limits who can run the free skill.** Claude.ai requires users to be 18+ (Anthropic consumer terms, effective 8 Oct 2025)
[42]; ChatGPT allows 13+ with parental permission [43]†. So the skill itself is for teachers and adult students; the prompt pack works in a
school-approved tool for 16–18s, run in class under the teacher's account policy.

## 5. The three to do first, and why

1. **E2, the teacher edition of the Diagnostic** (5–8 h). Least change of any artefact: teachers are adult knowledge workers, so no
   safeguarding, no minors' data, no procurement. It answers a measured gap (58% of teachers with no guidance on AI for grading, 2026)
   and turns the SME skill into a second audience at almost zero cost.
2. **E1 + E3, the Who-checks sheet with the five classroom stances** (16–24 h together). This is the precise gap from §2: the layer
   between "how much AI" (AIAS, two-lane) and the tool's built-in Tutor. It is the artefact a teacher can use on Monday, it is free, and it is
   what the pilot needs.
3. **E7, one pilot** (25–40 h over a term). The only route to a real case, and the only way to test the claim the literature has not:
   that students who *choose* a stance get some of the protection Bastani's GPT Tutor built in. Without it, any paid education offer
   would rest on borrowed evidence.

Hold E6 (paid workshop) until the pilot gives a case study; hold E5 until a teacher asks for it inside the pilot.

## 6. Honest constraints

- **Time.** 5–10 h/week with the SME track not yet earning. E2 + E1 + E3 + E7 is roughly 55–85 h, i.e. 2–4 months of the whole budget if
  nothing else moves. Recommendation: do E2 now (it reuses the SME skill), the rest only after the first paid SME assessment.
- **Schools buy slowly and pay little.** US districts sign student-data agreements before any tool touches students (SDPC's National Data
  Privacy Agreement, 28 state alliances) [44]†; COPPA's amended rule applied from 22 Apr 2026 with no school-authorization exception
  codified [45]†. UK schools run contractor checks under Keeping Children Safe in Education; overseas staff need certificates of good conduct
  [46]†. A solo foreign provider with no entity, no references and no insurance will lose to a free programme in most procurements.
- **Free, well-funded competition.** AFT academy ($23m), DfE modules, Common Sense, Experience AI, Khanmigo for Teachers, and the labs'
  own study modes are free. Paid teacher PD from an unknown is the hardest sale in the list. Money comes later and smaller than SMEs.
- **Minors mean safeguarding.** Design rule: Henrique never receives student data, never talks to a minor one-to-one, never hosts anything a
  student logs into. Every student-facing artefact goes through the teacher. Companion and Therapist uses are out of scope for under-18s.
- **The pendulum.** NYC, LAUSD and the Chicago pledge (Sept 2026) show that "AI in class" is now politically contested. Material that says
  "use AI well" can be read as pro-AI. Frame it as the calculator lesson: foundations first, secure tasks kept, the tool for what it is for.
- **Evidence size.** Bastani and Kestin are single-site; Kosmyna is a preprint; offloading studies are small or correlational. Claims should
  stay at "the design of the help changes the outcome", which both RCTs support.
- **Tool age limits.** Claude.ai is 18+ [42], so the skill is adult-only; the prompt pack must run in tools the school has approved.

## 7. What a pilot looks like

**Who.** One teacher Henrique already knows (upper secondary 16–18, or a first-year university module), one class (~20–30), one term
(10–12 weeks), one writing- or project-based subject. Remote: Henrique meets only the teacher.

**What happens.**
- Week 0: teacher runs E2 on their own AI use (30 min). Henrique and the teacher fit E1 to three assignments in the term.
- Weeks 1–2: teacher teaches the five stances (E3) in one lesson; students start the mode log.
- Weeks 2–10: three assignments with the Who-checks sheet. Each has one secure, no-AI component (a short in-class write or viva).
- Week 11: a no-AI transfer task on the term's content (Bastani-style outcome), compared with the same task given by the teacher to a
  parallel class, if one exists, or to last year's cohort (not randomized; say so).
- Week 12: 30-minute teacher interview; anonymous five-question student survey run by the teacher.

**Measures** (all held by the teacher; Henrique sees anonymized aggregates only):
1. Share of logged AI uses in Tutor/Mirror/Adversary versus Oracle/Genie, week 2 vs week 10.
2. Share of logs with a named check completed.
3. No-AI transfer task scores, pilot class vs comparison.
4. Teacher minutes spent checking per assignment, before vs during.
5. Student answers to "what kind of help did you ask for, and what did you check?" (can they name it?).

**Data and safeguarding.** School's data-protection lead approves first; parents informed by letter for under-18s; no student names
leave the school; no tool beyond what the school already approves.

**What it would prove.** Feasibility (teachers can run it inside a normal term), acceptability (students log without revolt), and a first
descriptive signal on whether named stances shift students towards Tutor use. It would give a written, anonymized case study for E6.
**What it would not prove:** a causal learning effect. One class, no randomization. Say that in the case study.
