# Which mode are you in?

Using AI on purpose: what kind of help you ask for, and who checks it.

<p class="small">Mode Fluency · classroom pack · CC BY-SA 4.0</p>

Notes:

**1 minute.** Before the lesson: print one set of stance cards per table, and one self-check per student. Prepare the slide 12
activity: print activity-handout.pdf (one half-page per pair), or make your own example (see the slide 12 notes). Students do
not need a device or an account for any part of this lesson.

Timings add up to 42 minutes, plus about 8 for the self-check at the end: 50 in all.

Say: "Today is not about whether you're allowed to use AI. You already use it, or you will. It's about something nobody has
taught you yet: what kind of help you're asking for, and who checks it."

---

## The calculator

In 1980, the US maths teachers' council recommended calculators "at all grade levels".

The machine took the arithmetic. The curriculum moved up.

<p class="small">NCTM, An Agenda for Action, 1980</p>

Notes:

**2 minutes.** Ask first: "Hands up if you think calculators made people worse at maths." Take two answers.

Then say: "When calculators came into classrooms, people had exactly the argument we're having about AI. In 1980 the US maths
teachers' council recommended using them at every level, and making problem solving the focus. The machine took the arithmetic, and the
human curriculum moved up to the harder thinking."

The quotation is from the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics, *An Agenda for Action* (1980): programs should "take full
advantage of the power of calculators and computers at all grade levels".

---

## Foundations first, then the tool

Calculators did not hold back maths skills when they were part of teaching and testing (54 studies, 2003).

The one harm signal: sustained use in Grade 4 "appears to hinder" basic skills in average students (79 studies, 1986).

**For which problems, when, and who checks?**

<p class="small">Ellington, JRME, 2003 · Hembree &amp; Dessart, JRME, 1986</p>

Notes:

**2 minutes.** Say: "Researchers looked at dozens of studies afterwards. Calculators did not hold students back when they were
part of how maths was taught and tested. The one harm signal was early: sustained use in Grade 4 'appears to hinder the development
of basic skills in average students', in the researchers' words. Foundations first, then the tool."

Point at the bold line. "That's the question for today. Not 'should we use AI?' but: for which problems, when, and who checks?"

Sources: Ellington (2003), a meta-analysis of 54 studies; Hembree and Dessart (1986), a meta-analysis of 79 research reports. Both
in the Journal for Research in Mathematics Education.

---

## Where the comparison breaks

A calculator is never fluently wrong.

An AI can be: wrong, in the same confident voice it uses when it is right.

Notes:

**2 minutes.** Say: "The calculator is a flattering comparison. Type 7 times 8 and you get 56, every time. An AI is different. It
can give you a wrong answer that sounds exactly like a right one: same confidence, same tidy sentences. And it doesn't stay below the
problem the way a calculator does. It can do the framing, the reasoning and the writing, which is the part you're here to learn."

Ask: "Has anyone had an AI tell you something confidently that turned out to be wrong?" Take one example. If nobody has, say: "You
may not have noticed. That's the point."

## Same AI, different results

| | Practice scores | Exam without AI |
|---|---|---|
| AI that gives answers | about **+48%** | about **17% worse** than no AI |
| AI that gives hints, not answers | about **+127%** | no significant difference from no AI |

Unrestricted answers hurt the exam. A tutor with guardrails largely removed the harm.

<p class="small">About 1,000 high-school maths students, Turkey · Bastani et al., PNAS 122(26), July 2025</p>

Notes:

**4 minutes.** This is the most important evidence in the lesson. Take it slowly.

Say: "Researchers gave about a thousand high-school maths students one of two versions of the same AI, or no AI. One version
answered questions, like a normal chatbot. The other was set up to give hints and ask questions, but not the answer."

Reveal the practice column. "With AI, practice scores went up for both. The answer version by about 48%, the hint version by about
127%."

Then the exam column. "Then they sat the exam without AI. The students who had practised with the answer version did about 17% worse
than students who never had AI at all. For the hint group, the researchers found no significant difference from students without AI."

Ask: "Why would a version that raised practice scores make the exam worse?" Let them get there: the answer version did the thinking
for them. Then read the bold line: unrestricted answers hurt the exam; a tutor with guardrails largely removed the harm. Same AI; the kind of
help made the difference. Do not say the hint version made students better at the exam: it did not hurt them, which is a different
claim.

Source: Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı and Mariman, "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high
school mathematics", *PNAS* 122(26), July 2025.

## Before you type: what are you expecting?

That expectation is your **stance**.

The same question, "explain photosynthesis", can mean:

- give me the answer to copy
- help me understand it before Friday
- check whether my own explanation holds up

Notes:

**2 minutes.** Hand out the stance cards now, one set per table.

Say: "Every time you open an AI, you have an expectation before you type: what do you want back? That expectation is your stance.
Most people never name it. The same words can mean three different things, and get three very different results for your learning."

Ask: "Which of the three on the slide do you do most?" Quick show of hands, no judgement. Then: "There are five stances on your
table. Let's go through them."

## The Oracle

**You expect:** truth to a hard question.

**How it fails:** confident plausible prose taken as fact.

**One rule:** ask for the uncertainty before the answer. Treat the answer as a claim, not a verdict.

<p class="example">"Before you answer, tell me which parts you are least sure about."</p>

Notes:

**3 minutes.** Say: "The Oracle is the answer machine. It's where almost everybody starts. The trouble is that a fluent wrong answer
looks exactly like a fluent right one, and if you asked because you didn't know, you can't tell the difference."

Read the rule aloud. "A claim is something you check. A verdict is something you accept."

Made-up example to give: a student revising for history asks for the three causes of a war and the date it ended. The answer reads
well and the date is a year out. The student who asked "which parts are you least sure of?" and checked the textbook catches it.

---

## The Tutor

**You expect:** a path to the answer, not the answer.

**How it fails:** being taught toward a goal you never stated.

**One rule:** state what you want to be able to do afterwards before the first question.

<p class="example">"I want to solve these on my own by Friday. Don't tell me the answer yet. What questions should I be asking?"</p>

Notes:

**3 minutes.** Say: "The Tutor is the one the hint version in that study was built to be. It's slower than the Oracle. That's the
point: the effort is where the learning happens."

"The way it fails: if you don't say what you're aiming for, it will teach you whatever it guesses you need. So tell it first: what
do you want to be able to do afterwards? A test on Friday, without a calculator? An essay you can defend in your own words?"

"If you remember one sentence from today, make it the last two on this slide: Don't tell me the answer yet. What questions should I
be asking?"

---

## The Mirror

**You expect:** my own thinking, clarified.

**How it fails:** flattery mistaken for reflection.

**One rule:** ask for the structure of your argument back, not for agreement.

<p class="example">"List the claims I make, in order, and the evidence for each. Don't tell me if they're good."</p>

Notes:

**2 minutes.** Say: "AI models may simply agree with whoever is typing. Ask 'is my essay good?' and you may hear yes whether it is or not. That isn't
feedback; it's flattery."

"The Mirror works when you ask for the shape of your own thinking: your claims, in order, with the evidence you gave. Then you can see
for yourself that claim three has no evidence at all."

---

## The Adversary

**You expect:** an attack on my plan, or my draft.

**How it fails:** critique of the wrong thing.

**One rule:** name what you are defending and what would change your mind.

<p class="example">"I'm arguing the town should build the bypass, because of air quality. I'd change my mind if the traffic just
moved to the villages. Attack that."</p>

Notes:

**2 minutes.** Say: "This one takes nerve: you ask the AI to find the holes in your work. If you just say 'criticise this', you'll
get comments on your spelling, or on a point you never meant to make."

"So tell it what you're defending, and what would make you change your mind. Then it can go for the weak link." Good use: before a
debate, before handing in an argument, before an oral.

---

## The Librarian

**You expect:** a synthesis of what is known: what has been said, not what is.

**How it fails:** confident retrieval of what was never said.

**One rule:** check the citation exists and says what is claimed.

<p class="example">Five sources on coastal erosion, with author, year and title. Then look each one up.</p>

Notes:

**2 minutes.** Say: "The Librarian is the AI as a research assistant: what have people said about this? It's useful, and it has a
known failure: sources that don't exist. Real authors with books they never wrote. Real books with quotes that aren't in them."

"The rule is simple and nobody does it: before you use a source, look it up. Does it exist? Does it say what the AI said it says?"

## Fluently wrong

One AI-style answer, printed. Your job: find what is wrong, and say how you know.

1. Underline every claim you could check.
2. Check two of them against your textbook or notes.
3. Which stance was the question asked in? What should have been asked first?

Notes:

**7 minutes. Prepare before the lesson.** You have two options.

*Ready-made:* print activity-handout.pdf, one half-page per pair. It holds a short answer about why the United States joined the
First World War and how the war ended. It is a made-up illustration written for this pack, not real AI output, in the confident style
an answering AI often has. It contains three errors:

- it says the United States entered the war in April 1916; it was April 1917 (and the Zimmermann Telegram it mentions is from January
  1917, so the answer's own timeline does not fit);
- it says the armistice was signed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles at 11 a.m.; it was signed in a railway carriage near Compiègne
  in the early morning and took effect at 11 a.m. (the Hall of Mirrors is where the Treaty of Versailles was signed, in June 1919);
- its source, "M. Hartwell, *Empires at the Brink*", was invented for this illustration; students should not find it.

The guide's appendix has the full text and this key. The topic is only an example: swap in a short answer from your own subject that you have checked is wrong; the three checks stay the same.

*Your own:* with an AI tool your school has approved, ask a question in your subject whose answer students can check from their
textbook or notes: a set of dates and causes, a worked solution, or "give me three sources with page numbers on [a topic you know
well]". Ask in the Oracle stance, just the question. Use an answer that contains at least one error you have checked yourself; it
may take a few tries. If your school has no approved tool, use the ready-made handout.

In class: pairs, one handout each. Five minutes on the three steps, two minutes of feedback. Ask: "What did you check it against?"
and "Would you have noticed if you hadn't been told to look?" For the third step: the question was asked in the Oracle stance; what
should have come first is "Before you answer, tell me which parts you are least sure about", and for the source, the Librarian's rule.

If you used your own and the answer turns out to be entirely right, use it anyway and ask: "How do you know it's right?" The answer
is the same: you checked.

Students do not use an AI themselves in this activity, so no student needs an account or a device.

## Who checks?

Every piece of work with AI help has a checker, named before you start.

**The checker is never the chat that wrote it.**

- you, against a source, your notes or your own working
- you, by doing one part without help
- your teacher, through the part you do unaided

Notes:

**3 minutes.** Say: "Asking the same chat 'are you sure?' is not a check. The AI that gave you the wrong date may simply agree with
itself, or change its answer because you sounded doubtful. Either way, you haven't checked anything. A check is something outside the
conversation: your textbook, the original document, your own working, or doing a bit of it yourself and comparing."

"And for your assignments, I'm a checker too. Some assignments will have a part you do without AI, in class or in conversation with
me. That part is how I check that you understand your own work."

If asked about detectors, speak for this pack, not for the school: "This pack uses no detector; the unaided part is the check."
Background, if useful: a 2023 study found detectors flagged essays by non-native English writers as AI-written far more often than
essays by native speakers (Liang et al., *Patterns*, July 2023). Your school's or exam board's own rules still apply.

## The mode log

| Date | What I asked | Which mode | What I checked myself | What I changed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 Mar | where my steps for Q4 go wrong, no answer | Tutor | worked example, p. 41 | the sign in step 2 |

One line, every time you use AI on an assignment.

<p class="small">Made-up entry, for illustration</p>

Notes:

**2 minutes.** Hold up a mode log sheet; there is also one at the end of the self-check. Each assignment that asks for a log gets its
own sheet.

Say: "When an assignment says 'mode log', you fill one line every time you use AI on it. What you asked, in a few words. Which mode
you were in. What you checked yourself. What you changed."

"The log isn't a trap. An honest 'Oracle, checked nothing' is more useful to me than a tidy log that didn't happen, because it tells
us both what to work on. In a tutor-only assignment: if you used the Oracle, record it; it counts as a deviation, and I'd rather know. I'll sometimes pick one line and ask you to show me."

## Three assignment modes

**A: No AI.** You do it all yourself.

**B: Tutor only.** Tutor, Mirror, Adversary or Librarian; not the Oracle. It writes nothing you hand in. Keep a mode log.

**C: Full use.** Use AI as the rules allow. Add a mode log, a short report on what the AI did and what you changed, and a part you
do without AI.

Every assignment sheet says which mode.

Notes:

**3 minutes.** Hold up an assignment sheet. Say: "Every assignment this term will come with this sheet, and it says which mode."

"A: no AI, the way you've always worked. That's the foundation, like the non-calculator paper."

"B: Tutor only. The AI can help you understand, question you and give hints. You may also use it as a Mirror, an Adversary or a
Librarian, with their rules. Not as an Oracle: you don't ask it for answers. It doesn't write anything you hand in. You keep a mode
log."

"C: full use. You can use AI the way people do at work, within the school's rules. You hand in a mode log, a few lines on what the AI
did and where you changed it, and you do a short part without AI, so we both know you understand your own work."

Ask: "Which mode do you think will be hardest? Why?"

## The one rule to take away

**Name the mode before you type. Then do its one check.**

You may use the calculator. You still sign the exam.

Notes:

**2 minutes, then the self-check.** Say: "If you forget everything else: before you type, say what kind of help you want. Then do
that stance's one check. Oracle: ask how sure it is. Tutor: say what you want to be able to do. Mirror: ask for the structure, not
agreement. Adversary: say what you're defending. Librarian: look the source up."

"You may use the calculator. You still sign the exam."

Hand out the self-check: about 8 minutes for both pages. If time is short, set it as homework. It is theirs; you do not collect it.
The log at its end is for their first logged assignment; hand out a mode log sheet with each later one.
